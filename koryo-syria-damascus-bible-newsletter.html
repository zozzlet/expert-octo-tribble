Syria  •  Biblical Damascus

The street called Straight is still there

Damascus goes back eight to ten thousand years, with a proper urban centre by the third or fourth millennium BC. Pretty old stuff.

Old enough, in fact, that it was already being named in scripture in the time of Abraham — long before Jerusalem became the city of David, or Bethlehem the birthplace of Christ. It turns up in Genesis, Kings and Isaiah before it ever becomes the setting for the most famous conversion in Christianity.

And a startling amount of it is still there.
“Arise, and go into the street which is called Straight, and enquire in the house of Judas for one called Saul, of Tarsus.”
Acts 9:11
That is a street name, given as a direction, some two thousand years ago. The Romans called it the Via Recta. It is one of the earliest streets in the city, it runs the length of Old Damascus, and it is lined today with shops, restaurants and markets.

It is also, as our blog puts it, still, well, straight.
Long before Saul

Damascus in the Old Testament

The city’s first appearance comes in Genesis, when Abram chases his nephew Lot’s captors “unto Hobah, which is on the left hand of Damascus.” Shortly afterwards Abram is complaining to God that, being childless, his entire household stands to be inherited by his steward — Eliezer of Damascus.

Centuries later comes Naaman, a Syrian commander sent to wash in the Jordan to cure his leprosy, who takes the suggestion about as well as you would expect.
“Are not Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel? May I not wash in them, and be clean? So he turned and went away in a rage.”
2 Kings 5:12 (KJV). Three thousand years on, still the definitive account of being offered advice you did not ask for.
“The burden of Damascus. Behold, Damascus is taken away from being a city, and it shall be a ruinous heap.”
Isaiah 17:1 (KJV). By Isaiah’s day Damascus was powerful enough — and troublesome enough — to warrant its own oracle.
The road to Damascus

What actually happened to Saul

In the mid-30s AD, not long after the crucifixion, a man named Saul was hunting down Christians. He had authority from the high priest in Jerusalem to go to Damascus, arrest those “of the Way,” and bring them back in chains.

Somewhere on the road in, a light from heaven stopped him and a voice asked why he was doing this. He arrived blind. A local Christian called Ananias was told, in a vision, to go and find him — which, given Saul’s reputation, he queried at some length before going anyway.
“And immediately there fell from his eyes as it had been scales: and he received sight forthwith, and arose, and was baptized.”
Acts 9:18 (KJV). He joined the community he had come to destroy, and became Saint Paul. This is also where we get “the scales fell from my eyes.”
“And they watched the gates day and night to kill him. Then the disciples took him by night, and let him down by the wall in a basket.”
Acts 9:24–25 (KJV). Paul tells the story himself in 2 Corinthians, and adds that he went out through a window.
Still standing

What you can actually go and see

 
The Street Called Straight
The Via Recta, running the length of Old Damascus, and one of the oldest streets in the city. Shops, restaurants, markets, and a name given as a direction two thousand years ago.
 
The House of Ananias
A modest house not far off Straight Street, where Saul was healed and baptised. It survives as the Chapel of Saint Ananias, still an active church and pilgrimage site.
 
The Chapel of St Paul at Bab Kisan
A stone chapel built into a gate on the south-eastern stretch of the old city wall, marking the spot where Paul is said to have gone over in the basket. A place of pilgrimage for centuries.
An honourable mention

The Umayyad Mosque, at the western end of Straight Street

In Saul’s day this was a Roman temple to Jupiter. Its outer rectangular walls still form part of the building standing there now.

In the late fourth century it became a Byzantine basilica dedicated to Saint John the Baptist. Damascus was taken by Muslim forces in 636, and for a while Christians and Muslims are recorded as having shared the site — before the Caliph al-Walid I had the church demolished and the present mosque built between 705 and 715 AD.

A shrine inside is believed to hold the head of John the Baptist, a figure revered in both faiths. And Islamic tradition holds that Jesus will descend at the Minaret of Jesus, one of the mosque’s three — though, to be clear, the Bible itself says nothing about Damascus and the return of Christ.

And that is only one layer

Syria was old before the Bible was written

The scriptural history is the layer most people know about. Underneath it there is a great deal more.
 
Palmyra
A Silk Road city in the middle of the desert, and the seat of Queen Zenobia, who took on Rome and very nearly got away with it.
 
Krak des Chevaliers
The Crusader castle T. E. Lawrence called the finest in the world. He was not wrong, and it is still standing.
 
Aleppo
The citadel on its great mound, the soap factories that have been making the same soap for centuries, and souks slowly coming back to life.
 
Bosra
A Roman theatre built of black basalt, so well preserved that it was used as a fortress for centuries without anyone needing to change much.
 
Maaloula
A village of pale blue houses stacked up a cliff, and one of the last places on Earth where Western Aramaic — the everyday language of Jesus — is still spoken.
 
If you’d like to see it

Christmas on the street called Straight

Damascus has had a Christian community since the decade of the crucifixion — Ananias was already living there when Paul arrived. Bab Touma, the Christian quarter, is a few minutes’ walk from the street in Acts.
