The eclipse of a lifetime is over Libya

Most total eclipses give you two minutes. On 2 August 2027, the shadow sits still for over six — the longest totality visible from land between 1991 and 2114.

The path crosses Spain, North Africa and Egypt. Spain and Luxor will be packed. The centreline, as it happens, comes ashore at Benghazi — where the skies are among the clearest on the whole track, and almost nobody will be standing.

For six minutes, day turns to night

The moon slides fully across the sun. The temperature drops. The horizon glows like dusk on every side at once — because you are standing in a 250-kilometre patch of shadow with daylight all around its edges. And the sun’s corona blazes out around a black disc.

At two minutes, that is an ambush. At six, there is room to actually look.
The path of totality

The best seat — with almost no one in it

Along the Spanish costas and the temples of the Nile, hotels are already filling and the roadsides will be shoulder to shoulder under the shadow. Both will be wonderful. Both will be busy.

Benghazi sits dead on the centreline, with a full six minutes and some of the clearest eclipse-day skies on the entire track. Eastern Libya sees a fraction of the world’s travellers. You get the centreline, the desert coast, and the space to simply look up.
 
Ancient Cyrenaica

You’ve come this far. So has 2,600 years of history.

Eastern Libya was ancient Greek soil — a string of classical cities along the greenest coast in the Sahara. Almost no traveller ever sees them.

Cyrene

A UNESCO-listed Greek city founded in 631 BC, spilling across green hills high above the sea. Its Temple of Zeus is larger than the Parthenon — and there is hardly a soul among the colonnades.

Apollonia

Cyrene’s ancient port, where a Greek theatre sits at the water’s edge and half the old harbour now lies drowned just offshore.

Qasr Libya

Fifty Byzantine mosaic panels almost no one has seen: the Lighthouse of Alexandria, the Nile in flood, and beasts real and imagined — laid in colour some 1,500 years ago, and still glowing.
One last thing

The man who first measured the Earth was born here

Eratosthenes of Cyrene worked out the circumference of the planet using nothing but a stick, a well he had only read about, and the length of a shadow. He got it right to within a couple of per cent, around 240 BC.

You’ll watch the sky go dark from the hometown of the man who measured the sky.
