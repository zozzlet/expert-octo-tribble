What a week on Socotra actually looks like

Life on the road. You'll travel in a convoy of 4WDs, three travellers to a car, alongside a full team of chefs, camping gear, and at least one English-speaking local guide. Roads range from smooth tarmac near Hadibo to tracks that barely deserve the name, so a 4WD is not optional. Most companies now travel with Starlink, so you'll likely have decent internet even out at camp, though a power bank or two is still worth packing. Toilets and showers, where they exist, are basic: a brick structure, limited water, and no queue-jumping.

Camping beats hotels, and it's not close. A full island circuit takes in the Diksam Plateau and its dragon blood trees, places you'd have to skip if you based yourself in a Hadibo hotel instead. Your tent is set up for you every evening, dinner is freshly cooked by your camp chefs, and honestly, the food might be the best surprise of the whole trip.

The Socotri people. Expect the hospitality of the Middle East with the relaxed pace of island life. Your guides and drivers will have you singing along to 90s tunes in the car within a day, and they light up whenever you try even a few words of Socotri.

Is it actually safe? Socotra itself is genuinely very safe, and has stayed separate from the conflict on mainland Yemen throughout. The real risks are outside anyone's control: regional politics and seasonal weather, both of which can shift flight schedules with little warning. Travel with an established operator and you'll be looked after if plans change.

Travelling respectfully. Socotra is far more liberal than mainland Yemen, but it's still part of Yemen, and the culture is conservative by Western standards. Dress modestly in Hadibo and other villages, keep swimwear to nature spots well away from towns, and be mindful with photos of local guides dancing or singing. Small courtesies like these help keep Socotra open and welcoming to travellers for years to come.
