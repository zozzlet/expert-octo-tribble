11 Things You Probably Didn't Know About Timor‑Leste

By Halef Gunawan  •  4 Sep 2026
The country's name translates to East East. Its only mall is a point of genuine local pride. And packages addressed to Dili still occasionally arrive in Delhi.
Timor-Leste became independent just a couple of decades ago, its tourism industry is still very much a work in progress, and it's one of the least visited countries in Asia. So here are a few facts that will make you the most interesting person at your next trivia night. You'll ace the geography round, at least.
01

The name of the country is "East East"

The most fascinating fact about Timor-Leste is the name itself. "Timor" comes from the Indonesian and Malay word for east, and "Leste" is Portuguese for the same thing.
Put together, the country's name translates to, roughly, "East East". Which makes Indonesian West Timor, by the same logic, "West East".
Timor (east) + Leste (east) = East East

02

Only a few have even heard of Timor-Leste

More than two decades after independence in 2002, Timor-Leste is still one of the most overlooked countries on the map. Dili International Airport connects to only a handful of destinations across Asia and Australia, and even the international postal system hasn't quite caught up.
Parcels addressed to Dili sometimes end up in Delhi. Top tip: write "Dili, via Darwin".
Read more on the blog →
 
03

No tourist traps or crowds

Wherever you go, you won't be fighting crowds for a photo. No pushy souvenir sellers pressing keychains on you, and no taxi drivers trying to overcharge you on the fare.
Tourism is still in its early stages and you'll rarely run into other foreign visitors. The few you meet are mostly there for work: oil, construction, UN postings, and a growing number of Chinese traders setting up shop in the Dili suburbs.

04

The currency is the US dollar

Timor-Leste uses the US dollar, with its own small coins for change. Thanks to its relative isolation and the strength of the dollar, prices run higher than elsewhere in Southeast Asia, which you'll notice if you're arriving from Indonesia.
Getting around Dili is affordable regardless. A mikrolet, the local minibus, costs next to nothing. There are no posted routes, though, so ask a local which one to flag down.

05

Most Timorese are trilingual

Portuguese and Tetum are the official languages, but Bahasa Indonesia works as an unofficial third, taught in schools and reinforced by Indonesian television. If you speak Indonesian, it'll get you further here than English will.
Timor-Leste shares its island, its culture and plenty of vocabulary with West Timor, just across the border in Indonesia.
Read more on the blog →
 
06

Timorese are devoutly Catholic

The Vatican, unsurprisingly, has the highest share of Catholics on Earth. The runner-up? Timor-Leste. Around 97 percent of the population are practising Catholics, making it one of only two majority-Catholic countries in Southeast Asia, alongside the Philippines.
Pope John Paul II visited in 1989, leaving a hilltop statue and his signature at the entrance to Dili Cathedral. Pope Francis followed in 2024. And Cristo Rei, the 27-metre Christ overlooking Dili Bay, was put up in 1996 as a gift from Indonesian President Suharto, intended to win over the Timorese. (It didn't work.)

07

Many Timorese are also Portuguese citizens

Dual Portuguese citizenship is available to anyone born before 1999 or with a Portuguese parent, which covers most Timorese born during the colonial period. After independence, Portugal offered university scholarships to build local skills, though a fair number of graduates stayed in Europe, and a sizeable Timorese community worked across the UK before Brexit.
Many have since come home, so there's a good chance you'll run into EU citizens in unexpected corners of Timor-Leste.
Read more on the blog →
 
08

A recent, painful history

Timor-Leste's road to independence was anything but smooth: the break from Portugal in 1974 and 1975, then the 1999 referendum that ended Indonesian rule, followed by full international recognition in 2002.
An estimated 100,000 people or more died as a direct or indirect result of the Indonesian occupation. It's recent enough that most adults lived through it, and many lost family. At Santa Cruz Cemetery in Dili, guides can point to individual graves, including four siblings killed within days of each other.
Read more on the blog →
 
09

Relationship with Indonesia: it's complicated

The 1999 referendum didn't sit well with the Indonesian government, and in the violence that followed, the Indonesian military left a trail of destruction across the country until independence was finalised in 2002.
Today the relationship has healed to some degree. It's a bit like having a big brother you have no choice but to live with. Trade, culture and family ties run right across the island, Indonesian tourism is picking up, and for a young population the conflict is slowly becoming a story passed down rather than a memory.
Read more on the blog →
 
10

There's only one mall in the whole country

Timor Plaza in Dili is, for now, the country's only real shopping mall, and a point of genuine local pride. It has everything young, trendy Dili wants: fashion stores, air conditioning, a supermarket and a food court.
Read more on the blog →
 
11

Dili is spotless

If you've spent time in other Southeast Asian cities, you'll notice just how clean and organised Dili is. Street cleaners work the main roads constantly, and it shows.
The markets are kept in excellent order too, including the Tais Market and the fruit market: spotless stalls and no aggressive sellers.
