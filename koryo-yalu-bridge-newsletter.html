Where it all began

One track, one lane, one very old bridge

Koryo Tours have been frequent visitors to Dandong, the main Chinese border city with North Korea, since the 1990s. For that whole time, the main artery connecting the DPRK with the PRC has been the Sino-Korean Friendship Bridge.

It’s a Japanese-built iron structure from 1943 (originally named simply the Amnok River Bridge) made up of a single railway track and a single-lane road, with a low load-bearing limit. Lovely to look at, not so great for moving lots of stuff. There has long been a need for something bigger.

The big plan

A shiny new bridge (in the middle of nowhere)

Back in 2009, Wen Jiabao, then-Premier of China, visited North Korea and an agreement was made to increase cross-border capacity with a new bridge. Construction on this 3km-long cable-stayed structure soon followed.

Unlike the old bridge, which sits right in the centre of both Dandong and the DPRK city of Sinuiju, this one reaches from the peripheral new district of Dandong onto somewhat emptier land a few kilometres south of the city.

Work began in 2011 and was mostly completed in 2014. And then the opening was delayed. And then delayed. And then delayed some more. No opening date was ever actually announced.

The view from above

The ‘Bridge to Nowhere’

In 2016 we flew over the bridge on one of the first commercial domestic flights in the DPRK, from Uiju Airport (just outside Sinuiju) to Pyongyang. From up there it was clear the bridge ended on the North Korean side in a pile of dirt, suggesting the nickname ‘bridge to nowhere’ was more than accurate.

[Image caption placeholder: The view from our 2016 Uiju to Pyongyang flight]
So, what happened?

Plenty of rumours, no answers

By this stage the bridge was apparently becoming a bit of an embarrassment for China. After sinking hundreds of millions of dollars into a project that was highly visible and very visibly unused, it was in danger of becoming a laughingstock.

But why the hold-up? As with most things in North Korea, there are plenty of rumours and no way to confirm them. Some say it was approved by the government and then hobbled by the military, with the gap between wanting more trade and wanting tight border security being one even this bridge couldn’t span.

Whatever the reason, it was clearly the North Korean side dragging its feet. China opened a huge customs house on its end, and infrastructure in and around Dandong New District was upgraded and expanded. The bridge, however, stayed firmly shut.

Stop, start, stop

Then the whole world hit pause

In 2019 things started moving again, with Xi Jinping himself stating that China would cover the costs of building roads and customs points on the DPRK side. They duly were.

But in 2020 the project, along with the rest of the world, ground to a halt. North Korea sealed its border from within for an extended period, one of the most severe reactions anywhere to the Covid-19 pandemic, cutting the country off from the outside even more than usual.

         
The latest

Trucks are rolling!

Now, more than six years later, there finally seems to be more happening. The roads are complete and the DPRK border is open once more (somewhat, anyway). There’s no tourism at the time of writing, but there is trade, and that trade needs a way in and out.

And in September 2026, it was reported that trucks were finally rolling across the bridge.

[Image caption placeholder: Looking across the Yalu from Dandong to Sinuiju]
What’s next?

A step in the right direction

More than fifteen years after it was started, and more than a decade after it was supposed to be finished, the New Yalu River Bridge finally seems to be open.

Whether it will ever be a viable crossing for tourists remains to be seen. But for this curious project to finally be used the way it was intended feels like a step in the right direction, and is almost certainly a huge relief to everyone who committed so much money, time, effort and patience to bridging the Yalu once more.
