Meet Ea-nasir, history’s worst copper dealer

Around 1750 BC, in the city of Ur in what is now southern Iraq, a merchant named Nanni sent his money and his messenger to a copper dealer called Ea-nasir.

The copper was bad. The service was worse. And Nanni, rather than letting it go, had his complaint pressed into wet clay in cuneiform — where it has now been sitting, fuming quietly, for roughly three thousand seven hundred and fifty years.

Guinness World Records recognises it as the oldest written customer complaint in existence. We think it is also the funniest.
“If you want to take them, take them; if you do not want to take them, go away!”
Ea-nasir, allegedly, to a paying customer

What actually happened

Ea-nasir was an alik Telmun — one of the merchants who sailed the Gulf to Dilmun, modern Bahrain, and came back with copper. It was a respectable trade. He appears to have been very bad at it.

Nanni sent a servant with the money. He was shown ingots of the wrong grade, told to take them or leave them, and sent home. Other messengers were dispatched to retrieve the cash and came back empty-handed — repeatedly, and, Nanni is careful to point out, by a route that ran through enemy territory.

What follows on the tablet is four thousand years old and reads like a one-star review written at two in the morning.
“What do you take me for, that you treat somebody like me with such contempt?”
“Is there anyone among the merchants who trade with Telmun who has treated me in this way?”
The universal cry of the wronged customer, circa 1750 BC.
“I shall exercise against you my right of rejection.”
Nanni, inventing the strongly worded letter.
And here is the best part

He kept it

When archaeologists excavated Ea-nasir’s house at Ur, they did not find one furious letter. They found several — an archive of them, from different customers, all making broadly the same point about his copper and his manners.

He filed his complaints. At home. For four thousand years. Nobody has ever satisfactorily explained why.

Why we like this story

Mesopotamia invented almost everything, including being annoyed

The land between the rivers gave us writing, the wheel, the city, the law code, beer, the sixty-minute hour and the three-hundred-and-sixty-degree circle. It also gave us the first person to write down that a supplier had wasted his afternoon.

That is what makes Iraq such an extraordinary place to travel. Not the grandeur, though there is plenty of that — but the sheer human ordinariness that keeps surfacing between the ziggurats. Shopping lists. Homework. School reports about lazy pupils. Someone’s hand, four thousand years ago, pressed into clay because the copper was rubbish.
 
Ur
Ea-nasir’s own city, and one of the best-preserved ziggurats on Earth. You can walk the streets his disappointed customers walked home down.
 
Uruk
Arguably the first city anywhere, and the home of Gilgamesh. Writing itself seems to have been invented here, largely in order to keep track of goods — which, given the above, is fitting.
 
Babylon
Hammurabi’s city, where the laws were written down and the consequences of shoddy work were spelled out rather more firmly than Nanni managed.
