STRUCTURE — WHY IT'S BUILT THIS WAY
-----------------------------------
You asked for 90% interest, so the tours do not appear until roughly
three-quarters of the way down. The running order is:

  1. The hook — Nanni, the bad copper, and 3,750 years of sulking
  2. Big yellow pull quote: "If you want to take them, take them..."
  3. What actually happened — the Dilmun copper trade, the messengers
  4. Three more quotes, each on a different Koryo accent rule
  5. The twist — he KEPT the complaints, a whole archive of them
  6. PRIMARY CTA: read the blog post
  7. The bridge — Mesopotamia invented everything, including being annoyed
  8. Ur / Uruk / Babylon, as places you can actually stand in
  9. The two 2027 tour cards
 10. "See all our Iraq tours"
 11. Sign-up

The single hardest ask in the email is "read the blog." The tours are
offered afterwards as a consequence of being interested, not as the point.

The Mesopotamia card's description ends "The Ea-nasir itinerary,
essentially" — that line is doing the actual selling, by connecting the
story they just read to the trip that visits Ur and Uruk.


FACT-CHECK NOTES
----------------
Verified before writing:
  - Tablet is UET V 81, British Museum 1953,0411.71, from Ur, c. 1750 BC
  - Letter is from Nanni to Ea-nasir
  - Guinness World Records lists it as the oldest written customer
    complaint
  - Ea-nasir was an "alik Telmun", a merchant trading with Dilmun
    (modern Bahrain)
  - Excavated at Ur by Leonard Woolley's joint British Museum / Penn
    expedition; several further complaint tablets were found in the same
    house
  - Quotes are Oppenheim's translation, "Letters from Mesopotamia"

Deliberately NOT included: where the tablet is on display right now.
Sources conflict — the British Museum holds it, but at least one source
has it on loan to the Zayed National Museum in Abu Dhabi. Since the email
never tells anyone to go and see it, this doesn't matter, but don't add a
"go see it in London" line without checking first.

The phrase "roughly three thousand seven hundred and fifty years" is
accurate for a c. 1750 BC tablet read in 2026.


DESIGN NOTES
------------
Colour split is roughly 75% white / 20% yellow / 5% other Koryo colours.

  Yellow         #F0B429   pull-quote panel, blog CTA button, tour card
                           rules, sign-up border, stripe
  Deep gold      #9A6F06   eyebrows, links, tour card text and buttons
  Cream          #FEF7E4   "he kept it" panel, both tour cards
  Cream hairline #F2E3BC   card borders
  Red            #E8112D   stripe, quote 2 rule, Uruk marker
  Teal           #45B5AA   stripe, quote 3 rule
  Purple         #3D3A8C   stripe, quote 4 rule
  Green          #2E9B4C   stripe

One thing worth knowing about yellow: it cannot carry white text, and it
is unreadable as text on white. So yellow is used only as a background
with near-black text on it (#2A1F02), and anywhere yellow would have been
a text colour it becomes the deep gold #9A6F06 instead, which clears
contrast requirements on white. That is why the buttons look different
from the Bhutan and Libya versions — the yellow button has dark text
rather than white.

The Koryo stripe leads with yellow rather than red, matching the approach
on the Bhutan (teal) and Libya (burnt ombre) newsletters. Note that Koryo
orange #F49B1B sits close to this yellow, so I left orange out of the
stripe here and used the other four brand colours instead.

Fonts are Circular Standard for headings and Karla for body, with
Helvetica/Arial fallbacks. Quotes are italic Karla rather than a serif,
since the brief is those two families only.

Dark mode via prefers-color-scheme plus Outlook.com's [data-ogsc] /
[data-ogsb] hooks. Cream panels invert to a warm dark brown (#2B2416) so
the Iraq identity survives; gold brightens to #FFD166. The yellow quote
panel keeps its yellow in both modes, which is deliberate — it is the
loudest thing in the email and should stay that way.

Mobile breakpoint 640px. 600px wide, ~36KB — well under Gmail's 102KB
clipping limit.
