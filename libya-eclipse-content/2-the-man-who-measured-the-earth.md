# The Man Who Measured the Earth Was Born Here

*In the third century BC, a man from a Greek city on the Libyan coast worked out the size of the planet using a stick, a well, and the length of a shadow. He got it very nearly right. On 2 August 2027, the century's longest eclipse falls across his hometown.*

---

There is a particular kind of pleasure in a fact that sounds invented and is not.

The first person known to have measured the circumference of the Earth was born in Cyrene, a Greek city on the coast of what is now eastern Libya. He did it with no instrument more sophisticated than a vertical stick. His answer was within a couple of per cent of the modern figure, and he arrived at it around 240 BC — roughly seventeen centuries before Europe collectively decided the matter was still open for debate.

His name was Eratosthenes. And on 2 August 2027, the Moon's shadow will pass directly over the hills where he was born, and hold the sky dark for six minutes.

We did not plan that. Nobody could have. But it is difficult to think of a better reason to stand in a particular field on a particular morning.

## A Greek city in Africa

Start with the place, because the place is the part most people have never heard of.

Cyrene was founded in 631 BC by settlers from Thera — modern Santorini — who had been driven out by drought and told by the oracle at Delphi to go and found a colony in Libya. They tried an offshore island first, made a mess of it, and eventually settled on a green shelf of high ground a few miles inland from the sea, with a spring running out of the rock. The spring was sacred to Apollo. The city took its name, according to the usual story, from a local nymph.

What grew there over the following centuries was not a frontier outpost. Cyrene became one of the great cities of the Greek world — wealthy, intellectually serious, and famous across the Mediterranean for a plant.

That plant was silphion, a fennel-like herb that grew only on this stretch of the North African coast and nowhere else that anyone could persuade it to. It was used as a seasoning, a medicine, and — most profitably — a contraceptive. It made Cyrene rich enough to stamp the thing on its coinage. It was also harvested so relentlessly that by the first century AD it was extinct, which makes it possibly the earliest recorded species driven to extinction by commerce. Pliny reports that the last stalk found was sent to the Emperor Nero as a curiosity.

By the time Eratosthenes was born there in about 276 BC, Cyrene was a city of libraries and philosophers as much as of merchants. It had produced its own school of philosophy — the Cyrenaics, who held that immediate pleasure was the only rational goal in life, which one imagines made for pleasant dinner parties. It sat inside the Ptolemaic Egyptian sphere, which meant a bright young man from Cyrene had somewhere obvious to go.

He went to Alexandria, and ended up running the Library.

## Beta

Eratosthenes was, by the standards of any era, absurdly capable across too many fields at once. He wrote on mathematics, geography, astronomy, poetry, music theory, history and chronology. He was the first person to use the word *geography* in the sense we now use it, and effectively invented the discipline — mapping the known world on a grid of parallels and meridians. He devised the sieve that still bears his name, the elegant method for finding prime numbers taught to schoolchildren today. He calculated the tilt of the Earth's axis to within a fraction of a degree. He compiled a chronology of the world from the fall of Troy onwards. He proposed adding an extra day every fourth year to keep the calendar honest, roughly two centuries before Julius Caesar got the credit.

His contemporaries nicknamed him **Beta** — the second letter — on the grounds that he was the second-best in the world at everything and the best at nothing.

It is one of the more backhanded compliments to survive antiquity, and it says as much about the Alexandrian appetite for taking people down a peg as it does about Eratosthenes. He was also called *Pentathlos*, the all-rounder, which is kinder and closer to the truth.

Either way, the Beta jibe has aged poorly. Nobody remembers who the Alphas were.

## The measurement

Here is the thing he is remembered for, and it is worth setting out slowly, because the elegance of it is the whole point.

Eratosthenes had heard — the story goes that he read it in the Library — that in the town of Syene, far up the Nile in southern Egypt, there was a well into which the Sun shone straight down to the bottom on one day of the year. At noon on the summer solstice, a vertical pillar in Syene cast no shadow at all. The Sun was directly overhead.

Syene is modern Aswan, and it sits almost exactly on the Tropic of Cancer, so this is true.

In Alexandria, several hundred miles north, that was not the case. At noon on the same day a vertical rod — a *gnomon* — still cast a short shadow. Eratosthenes measured the angle of that shadow and found it to be about one-fiftieth of a full circle: roughly 7.2 degrees.

From that single observation, and one assumption, the whole thing follows.

The assumption is that the Sun is far enough away that its rays arrive at the Earth effectively parallel. Granting that, the only explanation for a shadow in one place and no shadow in another at the same moment is that the ground beneath them is curved. And the angle of the shadow tells you *how much* of the curve you have travelled.

If Alexandria and Syene are separated by one-fiftieth of the Earth's total circumference, then the circumference is simply fifty times the distance between them.

That distance was reckoned at 5,000 stadia — reportedly measured by professional pacers, *bematistae*, who walked survey routes for a living and were evidently good at it. Fifty times five thousand gives 250,000 stadia. Eratosthenes later adjusted the figure to 252,000, most likely because it divides neatly by 60 and made his subsequent geography easier to work with.

## How close did he get?

This is where it becomes frustrating, and interesting.

We do not know precisely how long a stadion was. It was not standardised across the Greek world. If Eratosthenes used the Egyptian stadion of about 157.5 metres, his answer converts to roughly **39,375 kilometres** — against a true polar circumference of 40,008 km. An error of about 1.6 per cent.

If he used the Attic stadion of about 185 metres, the answer comes out nearer 46,600 km, which is about sixteen per cent too big.

Scholars have argued about which for a very long time and will continue to. What is not in dispute is the method, which is flawless, and the intellectual leap, which is enormous.

And it is worth naming the errors he was carrying, because he got the right answer partly by having them cancel. Alexandria and Syene are not on the same meridian — they are about three degrees apart in longitude. Syene is not exactly on the Tropic. The pacers' distance was approximate. Each of these introduces a small error, and they happened to largely offset one another.

He was also, in a sense, lucky in his fundamental assumption — but it was a *good* assumption, arrived at by reasoning rather than guesswork, and he knew what he was assuming. That is the difference between a lucky number and a piece of science.

The salient point stands. A man with a stick, a well he had only read about, and a clear idea of what shadows mean, worked out the size of the planet to within a couple of per cent, and did it two hundred and forty years before the birth of Christ.

## Shadows as instruments

What links Eratosthenes to the reason people will travel to Libya in 2027 is not just geography. It is method.

An eclipse is a shadow. That is all it is — the Moon's shadow falling across the face of the Earth. And the ancient world worked out an extraordinary amount by taking shadows seriously.

**Anaxagoras**, in the fifth century BC, got there first in principle: he argued that the Moon shines by reflected sunlight, that it is a solid body of rock, and that eclipses happen when one body's shadow falls on another. This was not received warmly. Suggesting the Sun was a glowing stone rather than a god was, in Athens, an actionable opinion, and he appears to have been prosecuted for impiety and driven out of the city.

**Thales of Miletus** is said to have predicted a solar eclipse in 585 BC, which stopped a battle between the Lydians and the Medes mid-fight and brought about a peace. Herodotus tells the story and it is almost certainly too neat to be true — nobody in the sixth century BC had a method capable of predicting where a solar eclipse would be visible. But the eclipse itself was real, and the tale tells you what the Greeks thought the sky was for.

**Aristarchus of Samos**, a generation before Eratosthenes, used *lunar* eclipses — watching how long the Moon took to cross the Earth's shadow — to estimate the Moon's size relative to Earth's. He got a sensible answer. He also proposed that the Earth goes round the Sun, which was ignored for eighteen hundred years.

And **Hipparchus**, a century after Eratosthenes, pulled off the trick that most deserves to be better known. He took reports of a single solar eclipse — that of 129 BC, which was total over the Hellespont but only about four-fifths partial as seen from Alexandria — and used the difference between those two observations to calculate the distance to the Moon by parallax. His answer came out at roughly sixty to sixty-seven Earth radii. The true mean figure is about sixty.

He measured the distance to the Moon by comparing two people's descriptions of the same shadow.

This is the tradition that Cyrene sits inside. Not mysticism about the heavens, but measurement — patient, geometric, sceptical, and startlingly good.

## What is actually there now

Cyrene today is a UNESCO World Heritage Site spread across green hillsides high above the Mediterranean, and it is among the largest and least-visited classical sites in the world.

The Temple of Zeus is the headline. It is larger than the Parthenon — genuinely, measurably larger — and you will very likely have it to yourself. There is a Temple of Apollo below it beside the sacred spring, a Greek theatre later converted by the Romans into an arena, a gymnasium, an agora, streets of Hellenistic and Roman houses, and a necropolis of rock-cut tombs running down the escarpment in their thousands.

Down at the coast is **Apollonia**, Cyrene's port, where a Greek theatre sits at the water's edge and perhaps half of the ancient harbour now lies submerged just offshore, visible through the surface on a calm day.

Inland at **Qasr Libya** are fifty Byzantine mosaic panels, laid around 539 AD and found by accident in the 1950s — the Lighthouse of Alexandria, the Nile in flood, personified rivers and seasons, and a menagerie of creatures both real and thoroughly imaginary, still holding their colour after fifteen centuries.

The region is called the Green Mountain — Jebel Akhdar — and it deserves the name. This is the wettest, greenest strip of the entire Sahara's Mediterranean edge: juniper and cypress, wildflowers, and a coastline of empty limestone coves.

Almost nobody goes. That is the recurring theme of eastern Libya, and it applies to a UNESCO city with a temple bigger than the Parthenon just as much as it applies to the eclipse.

## The morning of 2 August 2027

On that morning, the Moon's shadow will come ashore on the Libyan coast at around half past nine in the morning, universal time. Benghazi sits essentially on the centreline. Totality will run to a little over six minutes.

The light will go strange in the way it does — not like dusk, because the shadows stay sharp and wrong until very near the end. Then the temperature will drop, the horizon will glow orange in every direction at once, and the corona will come out around a black disc in a mid-morning sky.

And it will happen over the hills where a man was born who, twenty-three centuries ago, looked at a shadow on the ground and correctly deduced the size of the world.

You do not need the coincidence to enjoy the eclipse. Six minutes of totality on a clear desert morning is sufficient unto itself. But it is there, and it is real, and no other spot on the path of totality can claim it.

Eratosthenes is thought to have died in Alexandria in about 194 BC, in his eighties. The story — from the Suda, a Byzantine encyclopaedia and not the most reliable of witnesses — is that he went blind in old age, and, unable to read, stopped eating.

Whatever the truth of the ending, it seems a decent thing to remember him by measurement rather than by that. He worked out how big the Earth is by paying close attention to a shadow.

In 2027, an unusually large shadow will fall on his hometown, and a small number of people will be standing in it.
