# Why This Eclipse Is So Long — and Won't Happen Again Until 2114

*The 2 August 2027 total solar eclipse will hold totality for up to six minutes and twenty-three seconds. Most give you two. Here is the plain-language explanation of why this one is different, and why nobody reading this will see its equal.*

---

Most total solar eclipses are over before you have worked out what you are looking at.

Two minutes is the usual allowance. Two minutes to get the filters off the lens, remember to look up, register that the sky has gone the colour of deep dusk in every direction at once, notice the temperature drop on your forearms, and take in the corona — and then the first bead of sunlight returns and it is finished. Veteran eclipse chasers talk about the strange amnesia of it. People burst into tears and cannot afterwards say why.

On 2 August 2027, the shadow will sit on the centreline for **six minutes and twenty-three seconds** at its greatest point. That is not a marginal improvement. It is three times the usual, and it is the longest totality visible from land anywhere on Earth between 1991 and 2114.

Eighty-seven years. That number gets quoted a great deal, and it deserves an explanation rather than a headline. So here is what is actually going on up there.

## First, the coincidence that makes any of this possible

Total solar eclipses should not really happen. That they do is one of the more improbable facts about living on this particular planet at this particular moment.

The Sun is roughly four hundred times wider than the Moon. It is also roughly four hundred times further away. Those two numbers cancel almost exactly, which means that from the surface of the Earth the Sun and the Moon appear to be very nearly the same size — both about half a degree across, both roughly the width of a pencil eraser held at arm's length.

No other planet in the solar system gets this. It is not a law of physics; it is a coincidence of timing. The Moon is drifting away from us at about 3.8 centimetres a year, and in perhaps six hundred million years it will be too far away to cover the Sun at all. Total eclipses will simply stop happening. We are living inside a window.

But because the match is so close, it is also fragile. Push the Moon slightly further away, or bring the Earth slightly closer to the Sun, and the Moon is no longer big enough to cover the disc — you get an annular eclipse, a ring of fire, and no corona, no darkness, none of it. Nudge the numbers the other way and the Moon covers the Sun with room to spare, and totality runs long.

That nudging is exactly what happens on 2 August 2027. Both dials turn the right way at once.

## Dial one: the Moon's orbit is not a circle

The Moon does not travel around the Earth in a neat circle. Its orbit is an ellipse, and over the course of each month its distance from us varies by about fifty thousand kilometres.

At its closest — **perigee** — the Moon is around 356,500 km away. At its furthest — **apogee** — about 406,700 km. That is a swing of roughly fourteen per cent, and it is enough to change the Moon's apparent size in the sky noticeably. A perigee full Moon is the thing the papers insist on calling a supermoon; it is about fourteen per cent wider and thirty per cent brighter than an apogee one, though you would struggle to tell without a photograph of each side by side.

For eclipse purposes, a Moon near perigee is a Moon that looks bigger. A bigger Moon casts a wider shadow, and covers the Sun more emphatically and for longer.

In late July and early August 2027, the Moon is near perigee. Dial one is turned up.

## Dial two: the Earth's orbit is not a circle either

The same is true of us. Earth's orbit around the Sun is also an ellipse, though a much gentler one.

We are closest to the Sun — **perihelion** — in early January, at about 147.1 million kilometres. We are furthest — **aphelion** — in early July, at about 152.1 million kilometres. That is a difference of around five million kilometres, or a bit over three per cent.

Northern-hemisphere readers usually find this counter-intuitive: we are furthest from the Sun in July, in the middle of the northern summer. Seasons are caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis, not by distance, and the distance effect is small enough to be swamped by it entirely.

But it matters for eclipses. Further away means the Sun looks smaller. A smaller Sun is easier for the Moon to cover.

Earth reaches aphelion in the first week of July. By 2 August we have barely begun to move back in — the Sun is still very close to its smallest apparent size of the entire year.

Dial two is turned up as well.

## Put the two together

So on eclipse day the Moon is near its largest and the Sun is near its smallest. The Moon does not merely cover the Sun; it covers it comfortably, with margin to spare.

The practical consequence is the width of the shadow. The Moon's dark inner shadow — the **umbra**, the cone of full darkness — sweeps across the Earth's surface as an oval patch. On an average eclipse that patch might be a hundred and fifty kilometres across. On 2 August 2027 it will be around **253 kilometres wide**, and at the point of greatest eclipse closer to 258.

A wider shadow takes longer to pass over you. That is most of the answer.

## Dial three: standing in the right part of the world

There is a third factor, and it is the one people rarely mention, because it is the least intuitive.

The Moon's shadow does not crawl. It races across the Earth's surface at thousands of kilometres an hour. But the Earth is also rotating underneath it, in the same general direction — west to east — and that rotation partially cancels out the shadow's speed.

How much it cancels depends on where you stand. At the poles, the Earth's surface is barely moving at all. At the equator it is moving at about 1,670 kilometres an hour. Stand near the equator and you are, in effect, running along beneath the shadow, chasing it — and the shadow takes longer to overtake you.

The same logic applies to the time of day. When the Sun is low near sunrise or sunset, the shadow strikes the ground at a shallow angle and skids across the surface. When the Sun is high overhead near local noon, the shadow comes down closer to vertical and moves at its slowest relative to the ground.

The 2 August 2027 path runs across low northern latitudes, and the point of greatest eclipse falls close to local midday. Both work in the same favourable direction.

Three factors, all aligned. That is the eclipse.

## What that means on the ground

The path of totality on 2 August 2027 makes landfall in southern Spain, crosses the Strait of Gibraltar, and runs east along North Africa — Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt — before continuing over Saudi Arabia, Yemen and Somalia. Around eighty-nine million people live inside it.

Duration is not uniform along that track. It builds towards the point of greatest eclipse, in southern Egypt, and falls away either side. And it drops off sharply as you move away from the centreline towards the edges of the path — stand near the northern or southern limit and you might get a few seconds, or a grazing partial, having travelled thousands of miles for it.

Some representative figures:

- **Greatest eclipse**, southern Egypt: 6 minutes 23 seconds
- **Luxor**, Egypt: around 6 minutes 22 seconds
- **Benghazi**, Libya: around 6 minutes 9 seconds, sitting essentially on the centreline
- **Southern Spain**: closer to 4 minutes 30 seconds
- **Tangier**, Morocco: around 4 minutes 50 seconds

The shadow reaches the Libyan coast at around 09:30 UT, and the whole path takes a little over three hours to cross the planet.

## Why 2114, and what that claim actually means

Now the number everyone repeats. It is true, but it is worth being precise about, because precision is more impressive here than vagueness.

The claim is this: 2 August 2027 will produce the longest totality **visible from land** of any eclipse between 1991 and 2114.

The 1991 comparison is the eclipse of 11 July that year, which ran to 6 minutes 53 seconds and crossed Hawaii, Mexico and Central America. It is, incidentally, a close relative of the 2027 eclipse — the two belong to the same **Saros series**, number 136.

A Saros is a repeating family of eclipses separated by about eighteen years, eleven days and eight hours, after which the Sun, Earth and Moon return to nearly the same geometry. Saros 136 is currently producing the monsters. The 2027 eclipse is the thirty-eighth of seventy-one in the series. The next member falls on 12 August 2045 and crosses the United States with around six minutes of totality — genuinely long, and worth putting in the diary, but a shade under 2027 and a full eighteen years away.

After that the series begins its slow decline, and no other eclipse gets back above the 2027 mark until the twenty-second century.

So: not the only long eclipse you will ever have a chance at, if you are young and patient. But the longest, and the one that does not require you to wait eighteen years, or eighty-seven.

## What six minutes actually buys you

Duration is not just a statistic to collect. It changes the experience in kind, not merely in degree.

At two minutes, totality is an ambush. There is barely time to look at the corona properly before it is gone, and almost everyone who tries to photograph a short eclipse ends up remembering the back of a camera rather than the sky.

At six minutes there is room to breathe. Time to look at the corona and actually *see* its structure — the streamers reaching out along the Sun's magnetic field, the pink flicker of prominences at the limb. Time to take your eyes off it, look at the horizon glowing sunset-orange in every direction, notice Venus and Mercury out in a mid-morning sky, hear whatever the local birds decide to do about it. Time to look at the faces of the people standing near you. Then time to come back to the corona for a second, unhurried look.

Six minutes is long enough to photograph it *and* watch it, which at two minutes is essentially a choice.

It is also long enough for the temperature drop to become properly physical rather than a suggestion — in the desert, several degrees, arriving as a distinct cool breath on bare skin.

## Where to stand

The eclipse is a fixed piece of celestial mechanics. Everything else about the day is a question of geography and cloud.

Spain is the easy option and will be crowded accordingly. Luxor is the other obvious one, close to the point of greatest eclipse, and hotel rooms there have been booked out for a long time already. Both will work. Both will involve a great many other people, and a certain amount of jostling for a patch of ground.

Between them lies the North African coast, and in the middle of that lies eastern Libya — Cyrenaica — where the centreline comes ashore at Benghazi with six minutes of totality, some of the most reliable eclipse-day skies on the entire track, and virtually no one there to share it with.

That is a longer conversation, and we have written it elsewhere. But the physics is the physics: the shadow is widest and slowest across this stretch of the world, and the sky above the Green Mountain in early August has a long record of being clear.

Eighty-seven years is a long time to wait for the alternative.
