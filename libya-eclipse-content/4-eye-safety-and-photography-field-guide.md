# Eclipse Eye-Safety & Photography Field Guide

*How to watch the 2 August 2027 total solar eclipse without hurting yourself, how to photograph it without wasting it, and what to expect minute by minute. Print it, save it, bring it.*

---

Two things about eclipse day are worth understanding before you arrive.

The first is that the Sun does not become less dangerous because something is in front of it. For all but about six minutes of the morning, looking at the partially eclipsed Sun without proper protection can permanently damage your eyes — and it will not hurt while it happens, which is precisely the problem.

The second is that a great many people travel a very long way to see a total eclipse and then spend it looking at the back of a camera.

This guide is about avoiding both. The safety section is non-negotiable. The photography section is advice.

---

# Part 1 — Your eyes

## The one rule

**Certified filters on for every phase except totality itself.**

From first contact — the moment the Moon's edge first touches the Sun — right up until the last sliver of the sun's surface vanishes, you need proper eye protection. Then, and only then, during the roughly six minutes when the disc is completely covered, you may look with the naked eye. The instant the first bead of sunlight returns, protection goes back on.

That is the whole rule. Everything below is detail.

## What counts as protection

**Certified ISO 12312-2 eclipse glasses or handheld solar viewers.** Nothing else.

ISO 12312-2 is the international standard for filters intended for direct observation of the Sun. It requires the filter to block essentially all ultraviolet and infrared radiation and to transmit only a tiny fraction of visible light — roughly one hundred-thousandth of what arrives.

Things that are **not** safe, no matter what you have been told:

- Sunglasses. Any number of pairs stacked together. Not even close.
- Smoked or exposed photographic film.
- Neutral density or polarising filters intended for photography.
- Welding glass below shade 14 — and shade 14 is very hard to find and uncomfortably dark for this purpose. Just use eclipse glasses.
- CDs, DVDs, mylar balloons, space blankets, crisp packets.
- A phone screen showing the Sun's image — the phone is fine, your eyes above it are not.

**Where to get them:** buy from a reputable supplier rather than the cheapest marketplace listing. The American Astronomical Society maintains a public list of vendors whose products have been verified, and it is worth checking a supplier against it. Counterfeit glasses printed with an ISO number they do not meet are a real problem.

**We provide certified glasses to everyone on the trip.** Bring a spare pair of your own anyway. They weigh nothing.

## Checking your glasses before you use them

Do this in daylight, before eclipse morning, and again on the day.

1. **Hold them up to a bright indoor light.** You should see essentially nothing — a faint image of the filament at most. If you can see the room through them, they are not solar filters.
2. **Look for scratches, pinholes, creases or separation** where the film meets the frame. Hold them up to a bright light and look for any point of light coming through.
3. **If damaged, throw them away.** Do not attempt a repair. Do not use "just for a second."
4. **Check children's glasses fit.** Small faces and adult frames are a poor combination; a gap at the side defeats the whole exercise.

Old glasses from a previous eclipse are fine if they are undamaged and certified — the frequently repeated claim that they "expire after three years" applies to older materials and is not true of modern polymer filters in good condition. Judge them on their physical state.

## How to use them

- **Put them on before you look up.** Not while looking up. Turn away from the Sun, put them on, then turn towards it.
- **Take them off only after turning away.**
- Expect to see nothing at all through them except the Sun itself. That is correct.
- The Sun through a good filter typically looks white, pale yellow or slightly orange depending on the filter. All are normal.

## The serious warning about optics

**Never look at the Sun through binoculars, a telescope, a camera viewfinder or a telephoto lens while wearing eclipse glasses.**

This is the mistake that causes the worst injuries. Eclipse glasses are designed for unaided viewing. Any optic in front of them concentrates sunlight enormously — enough to burn through the filter and into your eye in a fraction of a second, faster than a blink.

Optical instruments need their **own** solar filter, fitted securely over the **front** of the instrument, on the sun side. Never at the eyepiece, where the light has already been concentrated and where filters have been known to crack under the heat.

If you are bringing binoculars, bring proper front-mounted filters for them or leave them in the bag until totality.

## What damage actually looks like

Solar retinopathy is photochemical injury to the retina. The critical fact is that **the retina has no pain receptors**. You will not feel it happening. Symptoms — a blurred or blank patch in the centre of vision, distorted shapes, altered colour — typically appear hours later, often the next morning.

Some cases recover partially over months. Some do not recover at all. There is no treatment that reliably reverses it.

This is why the rule is a rule rather than a suggestion, and why we brief it in person as well as writing it down.

## A safe alternative: pinhole projection

If you would rather not look up at all during the partial phases, project instead.

Punch a small hole in a piece of card, hold it up with the Sun behind you, and let the light fall on a second piece of card held in shade. You will see a small inverted image of the crescent Sun. A kitchen colander produces dozens at once and is oddly delightful.

You will also notice, if there is any foliage around, that the gaps between leaves act as thousands of natural pinholes and scatter crescent-shaped images across the ground. It is one of the loveliest small details of the partial phases and most people miss it because they are looking up.

# Part 2 — Your lens

## The honest advice first

**Six minutes is short. Frame it once, then put the camera down.**

We say this at every briefing and we mean it. The best photographs of any eclipse have already been taken, by people with better equipment and more practice, and they are freely available. What is not available anywhere is your own memory of standing under it.

Take your shots. Set something running on a timer if you want. But do not spend totality troubleshooting.

With that said — here is how to do it properly.

## The essential mechanic: filter on, filter off, filter on

- **Partial phases:** a proper **solar filter mounted on the front of the lens.** This is not a photographic ND filter; it is a solar filter, in the same class as your glasses.
- **Totality:** filter **off.** The corona is roughly as bright as a full Moon. Through a filter you will photograph nothing whatsoever.
- **The instant the diamond ring reappears:** filter **back on**, immediately, before you do anything else.

Practise the on-off action at home until it is muscle memory. In the dark, with adrenaline running and six minutes on the clock, you do not want to be working out how your filter attaches.

## Gear that actually matters

**Focal length.** On a full-frame body, 300mm gives you a small but usable Sun with room for the corona's streamers. 500mm is a good compromise. Beyond about 1,500mm the disc fills the frame and you will clip the corona, which is usually a mistake — the outer corona is the interesting part and it extends surprisingly far.

**A tripod.** Non-negotiable at these focal lengths. A solid one.

**A remote release or intervalometer.** Touching the camera at 500mm during a long exposure ruins the frame.

**Spare batteries.** Heat drains them and you will be shooting continuously for hours. At least two spares, kept out of direct sun.

**Plenty of card.** Shoot RAW. You will take far more frames than you expect.

**A second, wide-angle camera** on a tripod running on an intervalometer is the single best-value addition. Point it at the group and the horizon, set it going, and forget it. Those frames — people's faces, the 360-degree sunset, the landscape under the shadow — are often the ones you treasure, and they cost you no attention during totality.

## Settings

**Focus manually.** Autofocus will hunt and fail on a black disc. Use live view, zoom in to maximum magnification on the Sun's limb during the partial phase, focus carefully, then **tape the focus ring** and do not touch it. Check it once more before totality; long lenses drift as they heat and cool.

**Turn image stabilisation off** when tripod-mounted.

**Turn off autofocus, auto-ISO and any auto-exposure.** Full manual.

**Partial phases, with filter:** start around ISO 100, f/8, and adjust shutter to taste — typically somewhere in the 1/500 to 1/2000 range depending on your filter's density. Test this before eclipse day on the ordinary Sun.

**Totality, filter off:** this is where bracketing earns its keep. The corona spans an enormous dynamic range — the inner corona near the limb is thousands of times brighter than the outer streamers. No single exposure captures it all.

At f/8 and ISO 200, run a bracket from roughly **1/2000 of a second all the way to two seconds**, in full stops. That sounds excessive. It is not. The fast end captures the inner corona and any prominences; the slow end captures the outer streamers and, if you are lucky, earthshine on the Moon's disc. Later you can blend them, or simply pick the one you like.

Set the bracket up as a sequence you can trigger once, rather than something you adjust by hand while the sky is doing the most remarkable thing you have ever seen.

**Diamond ring and Baily's beads:** these are much brighter than the corona. Around 1/1000 to 1/4000 at f/8, ISO 200. They happen fast — shoot bursts.

## Dust, not cloud, is your real enemy

This is the Libya-specific part, and it is the one people underestimate.

The eclipse-day cloud prospects on this coast are excellent. What you will be dealing with instead is fine desert dust, and it gets into everything — lens threads, filter mounts, sensor chambers, the felt inside your bag.

- **Keep gear bagged until you are ready to shoot.** Not "mostly zipped". Closed.
- **Bring a rocket blower and a microfibre cloth**, and use the blower before the cloth, always. Wiping dust across a coated front element is how you put permanent swirls in it.
- **Do not change lenses in the open** if there is any breeze. If you must, turn your back to the wind, point the body downwards, and be quick.
- **Bring more lens cloths than you think you need.** They stop being useful once they are themselves full of grit.
- A cheap UV filter on the front of each lens is worth it here purely as a sacrificial layer.

## Practise beforehand

Genuinely — this is what separates good eclipse photographs from disappointing ones.

- **Practise on the ordinary Sun with your solar filter** to nail your partial-phase exposure and to check focus technique.
- **Practise on the full Moon** for the totality end. It is about the same apparent size and a broadly comparable brightness to the inner corona, which makes it a decent rehearsal for framing and for your bracket sequence.
- **Rehearse the filter-off, filter-on action** until you can do it without looking.
- **Time yourself.** Run a six-minute timer and see how much of your intended sequence you actually get through. Most people discover they have planned too much.

# Part 3 — The shape of the morning

Roughly, and in order:

**First contact.** The Moon's edge touches the Sun. Through your glasses you will see a small bite taken out of the disc. Nothing else has changed yet. This is where the partial phase begins, and it lasts over an hour.

**The long partial phase.** Slowly the bite grows. For the first forty minutes or so almost nothing seems different to the naked eye — human vision compensates remarkably well. Then, past about eighty per cent coverage, things start to turn strange: the light goes hard and metallic, shadows sharpen unnaturally, the colour of everything shifts slightly. Look for the crescent images under trees.

**The last few minutes.** The temperature drops noticeably. The horizon begins to darken in the direction the shadow is coming from. Wildlife often reacts — birds settle, insects start up. There may be a distinct wind.

**Shadow bands.** In the last minute or so, faint rippling bands of light and dark can race across pale flat surfaces — a white sheet laid on the ground is the classic way to catch them. They are subtle, unpredictable and caused by atmospheric turbulence. Many people never see them.

**Baily's beads.** The last of the sunlight breaking through valleys on the Moon's limb, forming a broken string of brilliant points.

**The diamond ring.** The final bead, blazing against the emerging ring of the corona. This is the moment people gasp at.

**Totality.** Glasses off. Filter off. Look up.

**And then all of it in reverse** — second diamond ring, beads, glasses and filters back on immediately, and a partial phase running back out for another hour or more, which almost everybody ignores.

# Part 4 — What to actually look at during totality

You have six minutes. Here is a suggested order, because without a plan people freeze.

1. **Just look.** Ten seconds, no thinking. Let it land.
2. **The corona's shape.** Not a fuzzy halo — a structured thing, with streamers reaching out along the Sun's magnetic field, sometimes several solar radii. Its shape depends on where we are in the solar cycle.
3. **The limb.** Look for **prominences** — pink and red flares of hydrogen at the edge of the black disc. In a good year they are startling.
4. **Look away from the Sun.** This is the instruction people forget and later regret. The horizon glows sunset-orange in *every* direction at once, because you are standing in a 250-kilometre patch of shadow and it is daylight all around the edges. There is nothing else in nature that looks like this.
5. **Find the planets.** Venus will be obvious. Mercury, and possibly others, become visible in the darkened sky.
6. **Look at the people.** Faces during totality are worth more than most photographs of the sky.
7. **Back to the corona** for a last, unhurried look — and get your glasses ready.

Then the diamond ring returns, and it is over, and you will understand immediately why people spend the rest of their lives chasing these things.

---

## The short version

**Eyes:** certified ISO 12312-2 filters for every second except totality. Never through optics without a front-mounted solar filter. Damage is painless and can be permanent.

**Lens:** solar filter for the partials, off for totality, straight back on at the diamond ring. Manual focus, taped. Bracket widely. Fight the dust.

**Everything else:** frame it once, then put the camera down and watch the sky.
