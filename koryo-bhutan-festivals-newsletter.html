To festival, or not to festival?

When is the best time to visit Bhutan — and why we deliberately go when nobody else does.
Bhutan’s high season is April to May and September to October. It’s also when the big tshechus and festivals are scheduled — which is exactly why almost every visitor comes then.

We do it differently. Not to be contrary, and not because those festivals aren’t worth seeing — they genuinely are. But they’re the version of Bhutan that everyone already gets.
Two ways to see Bhutan
Everyone else
April–May and September–October. Scheduled festivals, packed courtyards, a seat in the crowd.
 
Koryo
Off-season. Nothing scheduled, nothing scripted — and the moments tend to find us anyway.

Off-season doesn’t mean missing out

Travelling outside the festival calendar doesn’t mean our groups see less of Bhutan’s religious life. If anything, they see it closer up.

That comes down to our guide on the ground. Years of relationships across the country mean doors open that aren’t on anyone’s itinerary — a rehearsal here, an invitation there. We can’t promise any single one of these moments in advance. That’s rather the point.

Four from our recent trips:
March 2025  •  Trashi Yangtse

Tea with the governor, and a mask dance

We happened on the rehearsal for the tshechu at Trashi Yangtse Dzong — and ended up seated beside the governor for it. Tea, Bhutanese snacks, an unhurried conversation, and the traditional mask dance unfolding in front of us.
December 2025  •  Chele La Pass

Chanting at 3,988 metres

Crossing the highest motorable pass in western Bhutan on the way to the Haa Valley, we met a group of young monks. Chanting, drums and trumpets, the Himalaya stretched out behind them — and, of course, tea. One of the most spiritual hours we’ve had anywhere in the country.
March 2026  •  Trashigang

An invitation to the Great Global Peace Prayer

Our group was invited to attend the 15th Great Global Peace Prayer in Trashigang — by the governor, who had first hosted us for, you guessed it, tea and snacks.
Kyichu Lhakhang  •  One of Bhutan’s oldest temples

The Royal Grandmother at prayer

We arrived while the Royal Grandmother was praying — and were allowed in regardless. She received our bows humbly, then gestured towards the Buddha statue: our attention, she suggested, belonged with him and the deities rather than with her.

Why this only works in a small group

None of these were on an itinerary. None of them could have been.

A governor doesn’t invite forty people to sit and drink tea. A handful of monks at a mountain pass don’t stop for a coach party. These things happen because our groups are small enough to be welcomed, and unscripted enough to have somewhere to put an afternoon that suddenly opens up.
